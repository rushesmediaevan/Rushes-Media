A useful shoot brief tells the production team what the business needs to show and where people will see it. A mood board helps establish the look. The brief also needs to explain the job: introducing a team, showing a process, presenting a product or supporting a particular offer.

Start with the work you actually need to publish. Then agree on the shots, production time and finished files needed to support it.

## List the destinations before the shots

Choose the pages, campaigns or sales materials you are preparing now. A list of every channel you might use someday can make the shoot larger without making the brief clearer.

For each destination, record four things:

1. **The audience:** who will see the asset?
2. **The question:** what should it help that person understand?
3. **The placement:** where will it appear, and what space is available?
4. **The deliverable:** a finished photograph, edited video, crop or other agreed file.

For a homepage introduction, the question might be “Who will I work with?” For a service page, it might be “What does this work involve?” For a campaign, it might be “Is this offer relevant to me?” Those questions give the photographer and video team something concrete to plan around.

## Turn each destination into a capture brief

Here is a hypothetical example for a business refreshing its website and preparing one campaign. These are planning examples, not a Rushes client project or a required package.

### Website introduction

**Need:** introduce the people and environment behind the business.

**Capture:** a team or working-environment photograph, with alternative framing for the actual desktop and mobile layouts. If the design places text over the image, show the production team where the text sits. Check that the important part of the photograph remains visible in both layouts.

### Service explanation

**Need:** help a prospective customer understand the work.

**Capture:** a short sequence showing preparation, the work in progress and a useful detail. Identify what can be photographed accurately and what needs permission before the shoot. Avoid staging an example in a way that misrepresents it as completed customer work.

### Paid campaign

**Need:** communicate one approved offer to a defined audience.

**Capture:** photographs or clips built around that offer. Confirm the intended placements and their current specifications with the campaign operator before locking the shot list. Agree on framing, text space and video versions; one crop should not be assumed to fit every placement.

### Follow-up message

**Need:** answer a question after someone has expressed interest.

**Capture:** an agreed explanation from the relevant person, or a photograph that helps explain the next step. If you plan video, include sound, a script or talking points, editing and a destination for the finished video in the scope. A linked video and an image in an email are different deliverables.

## Build a shot list the crew can use

Give each required shot a destination, subject, framing note and priority. Separate essential shots from optional variations. This makes it possible to adjust the day without losing the assets the project depends on.

Group shots by location, lighting, people and equipment so the production team can propose an efficient order. The number of setups should follow the brief and available time. There is no useful universal minimum.

Plan photography and video together when both are needed, but budget for their different requirements. A still photograph does not require the same sound recording, performance, continuity or editing as a finished video. Agree on those responsibilities before adding clips to a photography schedule.

## Agree on the finished deliverables

Before booking, put the deliverable list in writing. Clarify:

- The number of selected photographs and edited videos included.
- Which crops, lengths and versions will be delivered.
- Whether the handoff includes high-resolution files, web-ready files or both.
- Whether captions, sound treatment and graphics are part of video editing.
- How selection, retouching, revisions and additional requests will be handled.
- Whether raw files are included; do not assume they are.
- The delivery date, folder structure and period of file availability.

For permissions and usage, ask the provider to document who may use the assets, where they may appear, and any agreed limits. Identify who will arrange permissions for the people, locations and other materials in the production. Resolve unanswered questions before capture rather than leaving them for the person publishing the work.

## Assign review and approval

Name a person who can consolidate feedback and a person authorized to approve the finished assets. They may be the same person. Make sure any other required reviewers are known before production begins.

At the shoot, review the essential captures before the relevant people or location become unavailable. After editing, check the final files in their intended layouts. Look for an awkward crop, unreadable overlay, missing caption or a detail that changes what the image communicates.

Keep approval of the asset separate from approval to launch a campaign. The budget, destination and handling of responses need their own checks.

## Make the handoff useful to the next person

Organize approved assets with names that help the website editor, campaign operator or business owner find the right version. Keep final files separate from selects and previous revisions. Include the agreed usage notes and retain a copy in the business's own storage.

For a [website or landing page](/web/), check the asset in the actual layout. For [campaigns](/campaigns/), confirm that the delivered versions match the approved placements. For [follow-up](/follow-up/), decide which message needs the asset and who maintains it.
